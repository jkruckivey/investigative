Module 4 — N: Narrow Down What Happened

Identifying Root Causes

Understand why it happened, not just that it happened

Knowing what happened is only half the picture. To decide fairly — and to prevent it happening again — you need to understand why. Ask a few simple questions of every finding.

Ask "why did this happen?"

  • Was training adequate, and was the person signed off on the task?
  • Were the procedures clear and available?
  • Was there a process or system issue that made the error likely?
  • Was the behaviour intentional, or the result of circumstances?
Key Principle

Not every issue is misconduct — but some are. The same result — a missed check, a wrong label, a repeated error — can come from very different causes, and part of narrowing down is telling them apart. Where the conduct is misconduct, disciplinary action may be warranted; where it stems from a gap, that gap needs fixing. Often both apply.

Common Non-Misconduct Causes

Before concluding "misconduct," rule out the alternatives:

CauseWhat it looks like
Training gapsThe person was never fully trained or signed off on the task.
Communication failuresDifferent people gave different instructions; expectations weren't clear.
Process issuesThe procedure itself is confusing, impractical, or easy to skip under pressure.
Human errorA genuine one-off mistake by someone acting in good faith.
System breakdownsEquipment, staffing, or workload made the correct action hard to perform.
Note
Why this matters: understanding the root cause points you to the right corrective action — a training gap needs training, an unclear procedure needs fixing. But finding a root cause does not replace accountability: where the conduct warrants it, disciplinary action may still be required, applied consistently. Root cause and discipline are not either/or — often you need both.
