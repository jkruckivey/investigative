Module 2 — F: Frame the Issue

Frame the Issue

Define the concern clearly before you gather anything

Every good investigation starts by framing the issue: turning a vague concern into a clear, specific, answerable question. Framing keeps you focused on what actually needs to be verified — and keeps assumptions out of the process from the start.

Separate Facts from Assumptions

A concern often arrives wrapped in someone's conclusion. Your first job is to strip that back to what is actually known. "This employee has an attendance problem" is a conclusion. "The employee was absent on the 14th and the call-in line shows no record of a call" is a fact you can check.

Reframe It

Instead of: "This employee has an attendance problem."

Frame it as: "Did the employee follow the attendance reporting process on the date in question?"

The reframed version is objective, specific, and answerable with evidence. It does not assume the outcome — it points you at what to verify.

Three Questions That Frame Any Issue

1. What concern has been raised?

State the concern plainly, without judgement or conclusion.

2. What do I already know?

List the facts you can already stand behind — dates, records, what was directly observed.

3. What do I still need to learn?

Identify the gaps. This becomes your list of what to verify in the next stage.

ⓘ
A well-framed question is neutral (it doesn't assume guilt), specific (it names the conduct, date, or standard), and answerable (evidence could confirm or rule it out).
