Module 3 — I: Investigate the Facts

Fact-Finding Conversations

How to talk to people so you get facts, not pressure

Conversations are one of your most useful sources — if you run them well. The aim is to help people tell you what they saw or did, in their own words, without steering them.

Use open-ended questions

Start broad and let the person talk: "Can you walk me through what happened?" Open questions surface more, and more honest, information than yes/no questions.

Listen actively

Let them finish. Don't fill silences or jump in with your own theory. What people add when you simply wait is often the most useful part.

Probe for details

Follow up on specifics: "When was that?" "Who else was there?" "What did you do next?" Details can be checked against the records.

Avoid leading questions

Don't put the answer in the question. "You saw him skip the check, right?" tells the person what you want to hear. Ask "What did you see?" instead.

Take effective notes

Write down what was said as close to their words as possible, during or right after. Good notes protect the fairness of the process and help you later when you document findings.

Tip
Reassure people it is safe to be honest. Let them know the goal is to understand what happened, and that cooperating with a fact-finding conversation is expected and will not be held against them.
